Methodology

The 100 MW / 1026 FLOP boundary ▾

Every row here cleared two filters before entering the registry: a ≥100 MW requested-capacity floor, applied at ingestion, and a training-scale check applied afterward — whether that capacity, run continuously for 90 days, plausibly clears 1026 FLOP under every scenario below, not just the reference one.

FLOPs are estimated across three named scenarios spanning a defensible range of real facility characteristics, not a single point estimate: PUE 1.15–1.40, rack density 20–50 kW, and sustained model-FLOPs utilization 25–50%, bracketing a reference scenario (PUE 1.25, 35 kW/rack, 8 GPU/rack at H100-SXM FP16/BF16 Tensor Core baseline throughput, 40% MFU). At exactly 100 MW the three scenarios land between 1.071× and 1.126× the 1026 mark — because lower PUE efficiency and lower rack density partially offset each other in this formula, which is why the boundary is reported as a range rather than a single verified fact.

GPU and FLOPs figures are illustrative order-of-magnitude estimates derived only from requested interconnection capacity — not a claim about actual hardware, and not verified against any facility's real power-to-compute conversion.

Geographic coverage ▾

North America has nine organized wholesale electricity markets that run public interconnection queues: PJM, ERCOT, SPP, MISO, CAISO, ISO-NE, and NYISO in the U.S., plus IESO in Ontario and AESO in Alberta. All nine are ingested here, each from the operator's own published file. The files are not equally complete, and a total that mixes them should be quoted with these limits:

  • CAISO — the source is the Cluster 15 request list (86 live requests at the 2026-09-19 capture). Earlier clusters and serial projects still in CAISO's queue are not in that file, so CAISO's rows are a floor, not its queue.
  • MISO — publishes no state, county, point of interconnection, or technology for its newest requests (its whole DPP-2026 cycle, plus a few others). The 83 live requests of ≥100 MW affected (35.9 GW) fail this registry's requirement that every row carry a state and are excluded, not guessed. Another 36 requests (8.6 GW) that state a location but no technology are kept, in the ambiguous tier.
  • ISO-NE — the public queue table does not expose study stage, so every live row shows as “Active”. Capacity is each request's net MW, and two requests sited in New York that appear in ISO-NE's queue only for capacity rights (both already in NYISO's queue) are excluded so they are not counted twice.
  • NYISO and AESO — publish load requests (NYISO's Load Projects, with an end-use code; AESO's Data Load and Industrial Load), not only generator-queue rows. AESO's list carries no developer names and shows “Under Review” in place of an in-service date for 27 of its 28 data-centre requests.
  • Developer names — of the five sources added most recently, only NYISO publishes the applicant. On MISO, CAISO, ISO-NE, and AESO rows, “Developer Not Disclosed” describes the source, not the developer.

“Continental” in the title now spans all nine organized markets, but not utilities outside them. The … on this page combine files captured on different dates: the five most recently added (MISO, CAISO, NYISO, ISO-NE, AESO) on 2026-09-19, the others earlier in August–September 2026.

Entity resolution ▾

Developer names are matched against a small, hardcoded list of known hyperscalers and wholesale colocation developers by fuzzy string match, plus whole-word brand-token matching for project-specific subsidiary filings (e.g. a filing under “STACK Danville Gen Power 5, LLC” rather than the parent company name). A miss against that list means not matched to a known name — it is not evidence the entity is a shell company, and it is not evidence of anything other than the name not being one this registry currently recognizes.

What this registry does not (yet) do ▾

This is a static, point-in-time capacity estimate, not a time-resolved load signature. A dual-layer signal — macro capacity factor over a facility's operating history, plus micro-scale power transients from synchronized events like distributed-training checkpointing — is a meaningful further direction and is not implemented here; nothing on this page should be read as a claim that it is.
